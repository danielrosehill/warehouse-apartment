# The Why

This system wasn't a hobby. It's a response to specific conditions in the Israeli
rental market that make frequent, involuntary moving both likely and financially
brutal. This page explains those conditions — because they're the reason the rest of
the repo exists — and then hands off to the system itself.

> **Note:** the legal points below are a layperson's summary with sources, not legal
> advice. Verify against current law before relying on them. Key statute: the
> **Fair Rental Law, 2017** — formally *Amendment No. 2 to the Rental and Lending
> Law, 1971* (חוק השכירות והשאילה), which added a residential-tenancy chapter,
> **§§ 25א–25טו**.

## The situation, briefly

Renting in Israel ~10 years, always paid in full, displaced repeatedly for reasons
unrelated to us — one landlord gave the flat to his daughter; another terminated our
tenancy after we asked him to fix a leak. This isn't bad luck. It's how the market is
structured.

## The mechanics: why moves are forced *and* expensive

### 1. No security of tenure
- Residential leases are, in practice, **fixed 12-month terms** (commonly "12 + option
  for 12"). At the natural end of the term the landlord is generally **free not to
  renew — no cause required.**
- *During* a valid lease a landlord cannot evict arbitrarily: grounds (e.g. non-payment,
  serious damage) plus a **court process** are required, and self-help eviction
  (changing locks, cutting utilities) is illegal. But that protects you only *until the
  term ends* — it does nothing about non-renewal.
- Israel has **no general rent control**. The old *Tenant Protection Law, 1972*
  (חוק הגנת הדייר) does give strong anti-eviction and rent protection, but modern leases
  explicitly exclude it (it's tied to the defunct "key money" era), so it doesn't help
  ordinary renters.
- Critically, the 2017 reform's **flagship provision — a cap on rent increases — was
  stripped out before passage**, along with a proposed rental ombudsman. So the "Fair
  Rental" law delivers **no rent control and no right to renew.** Its real protections
  are narrower: habitability standards, repair duties, a deposit cap, and mandatory
  written terms (these are *cogent* — a contract can't waive them against the tenant).

### 2. The move triggers a stack of costs at once
A single forced move can hit a household with the equivalent of **~3+ months' rent in
one go**, before rent on the new place even starts:

| Cost | Typical magnitude | Notes |
|------|-------------------|-------|
| **Security / deposit** | up to **~3 months' rent** | §25י caps cash-type security at the *lower of* 3 months' rent **or** ⅓ of total lease value — which, on a standard 12-month lease, works out to **3 months**. A ceiling, not a small number. Often via bank guarantee / deposit cheque / guarantors. |
| **Agent / broker fee** | **1 month's rent + VAT (18%)** | On an 8,000 ₪/month flat that's **8,000 + 18% = 9,440 ₪** (≈ US$2,500), commonly **with no receipt.** Legally it's the *landlord's* cost — see below — yet the tenant pays it. |
| **Moving cost** | e.g. **~10,000 ₪** (our prior move) | Movers, or DIY gear + labour. |
| **Lost income** | variable | If you're **self-employed** (we are), days spent packing/moving/setting up are unpaid. |

### 3. The broker fee: "banned," but paid anyway
This is the sharpest example of the reform failing in practice, and it's worth being
precise about, because it's routinely misdescribed.

**We have never once hired a rental agent.** In Israel, in practice, essentially every
apartment reaches the market through an agent **the landlord engaged** — and the
**tenant is made to pay that agent's fee.** Every agent fee we have ever paid was for
an agent working for the landlord, whom we never chose and never instructed.

Set against the law, that shouldn't happen:

- The 2017 law (**§25ט(ב)(3)**) says a tenant does **not** bear a broker fee **when the
  broker acted on the landlord's behalf** (מטעם המשכיר). Our exact situation, every
  time.
- **The workaround:** courts read the rental law as governing only the *tenant–landlord*
  relationship, not *broker–tenant* (e.g. appeal 55235-08-19, 2019). So the practice
  was to have the **tenant sign paperwork pretending they had engaged the agent** —
  manufacturing a broker–tenant contract that makes the charge lawful. In recent years
  even that fig leaf is often dropped; the fee is simply demanded.
- New **Real Estate Brokers Regulations (Ethics & Professional Duties), 2024** — part of
  the Ministry of Justice brokerage reform, **in force 9 March 2025** — reinforce the
  ban (with disciplinary liability) when the landlord is the client. On the ground, the
  fee is still charged to tenants.

**Net:** the statute puts the fee on whoever hired the broker (the landlord); the market
puts it on the tenant anyway — often as an untaxed, unreceipted payment of ~1 month's
rent + VAT. A protection that exists on paper and evaporates in practice is the whole
case for calling the reform a failure.

### 4. Comparison: jurisdictions that designed the costs out
The point isn't that moving is hard everywhere — it's that some places removed these
specific traps:

| Place | Security of tenure | Tenant-paid broker fee |
|-------|--------------------|------------------------|
| **Germany** | Often **indefinite** leases; non-renewal needs legal grounds | **Banned** for tenants — *Bestellerprinzip*: whoever hires the agent pays (the landlord) |
| **Much of the EU** | Longer terms / stronger non-renewal protection common | Frequently capped or restricted |
| **Israel** | Fixed 12-month terms, no right to renew, no rent control | ~1 month + VAT; nominally the landlord's if the broker is theirs, but routinely shifted to the tenant |

Same activity, very different exposure. In Israel the structure pushes the market's
instability — financial and logistical — onto the tenant.

## The response

You can't fix the law from your living room. But you *can* change what a move costs
**you** — in money, time, and back pain — by borrowing the tools industry already built
for moving things efficiently and repeatedly.

That's the rest of this repository. Belongings live in standardized, interlocking
industrial containers that double as moving boxes and permanent storage; those tile
onto pallets; pallets move on jacks. Each move gets cheaper and less punishing than the
last, because the "boxes" already exist and already hold our lives.
